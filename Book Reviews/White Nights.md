# White Nights by Fyodor Dostoevesky - Escapism of Dostoevesky

This book is about escapism. That's what I think it is. The Dreamer (the main character) is an escapist. The defintion of Escapism: the habit of turning away from real life, routine, or stress into imaginative activities, media, or entertainment. This blog is to prove that Dostoevesky himself was an escapist, or had tendencies. Here I put two pages from my diary verbatim:

Dostoevesky writes about a Dreamer, Dreamer who is an escapist, in his book - White Nights. He was 26-27 years old when he wrote it - very young. The detail with which he writes it is striking and I suspect he was an "escapist" himself.
"Both Turkish and Non-Turkish chairs" - Dreamer notices such a minute detail. It was about a family going to their "dacha" with chairs of these two kinds. Why did Dostoevesky show the sharp observations of the Dreamer? Because by showing the Dreamer's striking observation power Dostoevesky is trying to say that escapists are sharp observers, not ignorant as the first belief may be.
It is only this "heavy volume" of real world observations, minutest, slightest and smallest they may be, that feed their escapist inner world. So, Dostoevesky is saying that escapists are NOT absent minded, they are very fine observers - vice versa is not true in general.
Another example of this is, in the same book, the Dreamer notices color changes, construction changes of buildings. Any change in a building, the Dreamer notices. And then builds an internal monologue with the buildings. Again both
1. Sharp Observation of an escapist, and
2. Real world observation, small, big whatever, but in heavy volumes feeding his inner [rich] world.
Both (1) and (2) are confirmed in this example too.

How could Dostoevesky know all this? The answer is, he was an escapist himself; an escapist, a dreamer, and a young one.

You may confirm the same in his other book "Crime and Punishment". At one poin in the book, Raskalnikov says something like:
> "...you talk too much so you don't can't do much, or you do not much so you talk too much"
Something like that...[The quote is Not exact; from memory]. Again, Dostoevesky is hinting to an escapist tendency in the main character of his book.

I'll keep looking for more such details that hint towards his escapist tendency in his books. I am sure he's put plenty in other books. He was mid 40s during "Crime and Punishment", so here escapism is mild through a monologue. 26-27 during White Nights, so more escapism [so much so, that the entire book is on escapism]. So books he wrote in his younger years should have more and potentially direct evidences of escapism. In his older years, he must have matured and gotten over it, and so he books like "Crime and Punishment" have lesser evidences of the same.
